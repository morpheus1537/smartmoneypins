The 50/30/20 Budget Made Simple (with a Free Printable Template)

Updated October 29, 2026

If you have ever opened a budgeting app, stared at 40 spending categories and closed it again, the 50/30/20 budget is for you. It has exactly three buckets, it fits on one sheet of paper, and it works whether you earn $2,000 a month or $10,000. This guide walks you through it step by step, shows you what goes where, and gives you a free printable worksheet so you can set it up tonight.

What is the 50/30/20 budget?

The idea was popularized by Senator Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in their book All Your Worth. It splits your after-tax income into three parts:

  • 50% for needs: the things you must pay to live and work.
  • 30% for wants: the things that make life enjoyable but that you could cut if you had to.
  • 20% for savings and debt payoff: money that makes your future self better off.

That is the whole system. Instead of tracking every coffee, you only need to know which of three buckets a dollar belongs in, and whether each bucket is roughly on target.

Step 1: Find your real monthly take-home pay

Use the amount that actually lands in your bank account, not your salary. If you are paid every two weeks, multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12 to get a monthly average. If your income changes month to month (freelancing, tips, gig work), look at the last three to six months and use your lowest typical month. Budgeting from a low month means good months become a bonus instead of a trap.

One subtle point: if money for retirement or health insurance is already taken out of your paycheck before it reaches you, you can either leave it out entirely, or add it back to your income and count it in the right bucket (retirement in savings, insurance in needs). Either way is fine. Just be consistent.

Step 2: Do the quick math

Multiply your take-home pay by 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. For example, with a take-home pay of $3,400 a month:

  • Needs: $3,400 × 0.50 = $1,700
  • Wants: $3,400 × 0.30 = $1,020
  • Savings and debt: $3,400 × 0.20 = $680

Write those three numbers at the top of the worksheet. They are your targets, not rules carved in stone.

Step 3: Sort your spending into the three buckets

Pull up the last month of bank and card statements. Go line by line and mark each expense N, W or S. This is the part people overthink, so here is a simple test: if you lost your job tomorrow, would you still have to pay this? If yes, it is a need. If you would cancel or shrink it, it is a want.

Common needs (50%)

  • Rent or mortgage, renters or homeowners insurance
  • Utilities: electricity, water, heat, basic internet
  • Groceries (the basics, not takeout)
  • Transportation to work: car payment, gas, insurance, transit pass
  • Health insurance, prescriptions, necessary medical costs
  • Childcare so you can work
  • Minimum payments on all debts

Common wants (30%)

  • Restaurants, takeout, delivery and coffee runs
  • Streaming services and subscriptions
  • Shopping for clothes beyond the basics, hobbies, decor
  • Travel, concerts, gym memberships you could replace with free workouts
  • The upgrade part of a need: if a basic phone plan costs $30 and yours costs $80, the extra $50 is a want

Savings and debt payoff (20%)

  • Emergency fund contributions
  • Retirement savings (401(k), IRA)
  • Extra payments on debt above the minimum
  • Saving for a goal: a car, a home down payment, a move

Step 4: Compare and adjust

Add up each bucket and compare it to your targets. Almost nobody hits 50/30/20 on the first try, and that is normal. Here is how to read the results:

Needs are over 50%. This is very common, especially in high-rent cities. Don't panic. Needs are the hardest bucket to change quickly, so for now, borrow from wants. A 60/20/20 split is still a solid budget. Over the longer term, look at the big three: housing, transportation and food. A cheaper phone plan, shopping insurance once a year, or a roommate can move the needle more than skipping lattes.

Wants are over 30%. Pick the two or three biggest want categories and set a monthly cap for each. Cancel subscriptions you haven't used in the last month. You don't need to cut everything you enjoy; the point of the 30% is permission to spend on what matters to you.

Savings are under 20%. Start with whatever you can, even 5%, and raise it by 1–2% every few months or whenever you get a raise. Consistency beats size.

Step 5: Automate it

The easiest budget is the one you don't have to think about. A simple setup:

  1. On payday, an automatic transfer moves your 20% into a separate savings account.
  2. Bills for needs are on autopay from your main checking account.
  3. Your want money stays in checking (or on one card) and whatever is there is yours to spend guilt-free.

Some people go one step further and use a second checking account just for wants, so they can see at a glance how much fun money is left.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using gross pay. Your budget should be built on what you actually take home.
  • Calling every want a need. Be honest. Cable TV is a want. A $70 dinner is a want even if you were hungry.
  • Forgetting irregular costs. Car registration, annual subscriptions, holiday gifts and birthdays all come around. Add up a year's worth, divide by 12, and set that amount aside each month.
  • Quitting after one bad month. Overspending once doesn't break the system. Just start the next month fresh.

Is 50/30/20 right for you?

It is ideal if you're new to budgeting, if detailed tracking makes you miserable, or if you want a quick check on whether your spending is in a healthy range. If you have high-interest debt, you may want to temporarily shift to something like 50/20/30, with 30% going to savings and debt, until the debt is gone. If you have a very high cost of living, 60/20/20 might be more realistic. The percentages are a starting point; the habit of giving every dollar a job is what matters.

Quick start checklist

  1. Find your monthly take-home pay.
  2. Multiply by 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2.
  3. Sort last month's spending into needs, wants and savings.
  4. Compare, then pick one or two adjustments.
  5. Automate your savings transfer for payday.
  6. Print the worksheet and review it once a month.
